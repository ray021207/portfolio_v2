Wildfire smoke detection · Computer vision

How early can a new camera spot wildfire smoke?

I tested whether watching how a scene changes over time can detect smoke earlier on unfamiliar cameras, and how many false alerts that speed costs.

Testing on cameras the model hadn’t seen

I split the data by fire day and reserved seven entire camera sites for a separate test. I compared a single-frame detector with a model that also saw the difference from the previous frame. Both used overlapping tiles to preserve small plumes. I selected thresholds on validation data and froze them before testing.

Faster alerts came with a trade-off

Across the 19 fires at held-out sites, the single-frame model detected 68% within 10 minutes, with a median detection time of five minutes after the first visible smoke label and 95.4 estimated false alerts per camera-day. Adding temporal differencing raised the 10-minute detection rate to 84% and cut the median to two minutes, but raised false alerts to 116.3 per camera-day.

Requiring detections in three of the last five frames brought the temporal model to 38.1 estimated false alerts per camera-day, with a three-minute median and 74% of fires detected within 10 minutes.

What the benchmark can tell us

There were only about 13 smoke-free camera-hours in each test set. The daily false-alert figures are extrapolations from those short windows and have wide confidence intervals. Haze and clouds were common false-alert sources. At a strict validation budget of one false alert per camera-day, every tested model missed most fires. The model would need longer negative recordings, broader regional coverage and additional validation before field use.
