The problem isn't visible from the ground

As groundwater is extracted, compacting aquifer layers can cause the land surface to subside, permanently reducing some storage capacity. In southern Arizona, those changes affect infrastructure, farmland, and communities.

The team set out to make this slow-moving process legible without flattening away scientific uncertainty.

What the product actually shows

  • Satellite-derived ground-motion patterns for five study areas, using roughly a decade of Sentinel-1 measurements.
  • Early NASA NISAR radar comparisons, with their short time record treated as a limitation rather than a headline rate.
  • Per-area reports that connect measured ground motion with geological and groundwater context.
  • Two reading modes: plain language for a general audience and research details for technical users.
  • Maps, visualizations, data-source references and uncertainty statements designed to make findings inspectable.

What changed when the data didn't cooperate

The original direction emphasized NISAR's first year. The available record turned out too short and noisy to responsibly estimate a long-term subsidence rate. The team changed course: use the longer Sentinel-1 record for headline rates, then assess how NISAR agrees with it.

A candidate West Valley area also failed to show a convincing sinking signal, so the project explicitly notes it as checked rather than manufacturing a more dramatic story.

Scientific and attribution limits: The website is a team NASA Space Apps hackathon project, not an official NASA product. Early NISAR figures are provisional. Pumping and subsidence associations do not establish causation, and radar measures slanted line-of-sight movement rather than vertical motion directly.

Why it belongs in my portfolio

The best part of a public-facing science tool is making room for uncertainty. Design should help someone understand what the data supports, not make a fragile conclusion look definitive.
