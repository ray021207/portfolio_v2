Why separate the planners?

A mobile robot in a partly unknown world faces at least three questions: Which geometric route is short and collision-free? In what order should tasks happen? What should it do when immediate conditions do not match the global plan?

An A* planner handles geometric routes, PDDL represents symbolic task structure, and a local Q-learning policy reacts to nearby situations. That separation makes the reasoning easier to inspect.

The behavior that broke first

Early reinforcement-learning experiments rewarded apparent progress without necessarily rewarding movement along the route selected by the global planner. The result was a local policy that could wander while still maximizing its own reward.

Reward shaping based on progress along the A* path improved reported path efficiency by roughly 20% in Gazebo. It was a lesson in aligning local rewards with higher-level goals, not a claim of solving general robot navigation.

What the project demonstrates

The work combines environment mapping from LiDAR, geometric motion planning, symbolic sequencing and reactive policy learning. The architecture is the contribution: each method does the part it is suited for.

The quoted improvement is from a simulator experiment. Physical navigation performance, map diversity and broader statistical replication would need separate evaluation.

What this evidence supports

Reported performance refers to Gazebo experiments in the prior project write-up; no independent field-robot validation is asserted.
