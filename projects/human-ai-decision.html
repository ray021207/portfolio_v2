Does having AI help people decide better?

A 22-participant experiment testing outcomes, workload and confidence rather than assuming access to an assistant must help.

The question

AI tools are available in more decision workflows, but availability is not the same as impact. In this study, participants played the Spent financial-survival simulation, with one group having access to ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot.

The evaluation asked whether AI access changed decision performance, reported confidence or perceived workload.

Method and evidence

The ASU human-factors project used a randomized between-subjects design with 22 participants and a comparison between AI-access and no-AI-access conditions. Workload was assessed through a NASA-TLX-style instrument and statistical comparison.

The prior project write-up reports no statistically significant difference across the three tracked outcomes. A null result was preserved rather than reframed as success.

How I interpret it

AI assistance depends on trust, fit with the task and the ability to understand a suggestion in context. Lee and See's trust-calibration framing is one useful interpretation, though this small study does not by itself isolate that mechanism.

The study is exploratory. A larger sample and task-level behavioral logs would help distinguish low usage from ineffective recommendations.

What this evidence supports

Academic study with N=22. No statistically significant group differences reported; this is not evidence that AI never helps decision-making.
