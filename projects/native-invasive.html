What I was trying to learn

Camera traps are appealing because they can observe animals without someone standing nearby. But a model that recognizes wildlife at one site may fail when the habitat, camera, lighting or species population changes.

I wanted to test that gap directly, rather than assume strong performance on a familiar validation split predicted performance in the field.

The evaluation mattered more than another training run

I assembled a six-country benchmark, audited labels and fixed mistakes in the pipeline, including taxonomic mislabels and a bounding-box bug. To reduce the most obvious leakage path, I split the data by camera rather than letting near-identical frames from the same device appear on both sides.

Then I tested performance on a different camera network in Hawaiʻi. The goal was not to prove one model always wins, but to see which assumptions stopped holding once the conditions changed.

What broke

The fine-tuned classifier looked excellent in-distribution. On the unfamiliar images, it failed to recognize any of the 21 invasive-class examples. A zero-shot approach using BioCLIP and a location-scoped candidate species list recognized 14 of those 21.

That suggests high validation accuracy was not measuring the kind of generalization that matters in deployment. The result also shows why constraining predictions with ecological context can help—without making that a universal rule.

Important limitation: This Hawaiʻi invasive-class subset contains only 21 images. These figures are informative failure evidence, not a statistically complete assessment of every habitat, camera, or species.

What I'd do next

  • Collect more out-of-distribution examples from multiple camera networks and ecosystems.
  • Report per-species recall and calibration, not only overall accuracy.
  • Test open-set detection and confidence-based human review when the model sees something unfamiliar.
  • Separate the advantages of ecological candidate lists from the model architecture itself.

For conservation, an error often matters most when the model says nothing unusual happened. That's why I want to keep testing what these systems miss.
