The use case

A fall-alert wearable must be simple to wear, reliably sense concerning movement, and avoid making everyday activity feel like an emergency. That requires engineering decisions framed by accessibility rather than sensor novelty.

How the team worked

At Tikkun Olam Makers at ASU, I led a five-person cross-functional team developing a smart ring prototype. Work spanned hardware, firmware, sensor integration and an ML-based alert concept, with iteration across project sprints.

What we learned

Comfort, battery life, detection latency, false alarms and a usable notification experience all affect whether an assistive concept is genuinely helpful. A functioning prototype is a meaningful milestone, but it does not settle real-world reliability.

The boundaries

This was a student/community assistive-technology project, not a separate paid employer. There is no publicly documented clinical evaluation or certified fall-detection benchmark.

Evidence & limits

Student prototype and team-leadership experience; not a validated medical or emergency-response device.
